Theory

For an interactive demonstration of the concepts discussed here, see the Applet.

1. Problem setting

Consider a surface represented as the graph of a scalar function defined over a rectangular planar domain. More precisely, let

\[ f : D \subset \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R} \]

be a sufficiently regular function, where \(D\) is a rectangle in the \(xz\)-plane. The reference surface is then the subset of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) given by

\[ \Sigma = \{(x, f(x,z), z) \mid (x,z) \in D\}. \]

In the application, this surface is taken as the initial datum and may be generated either by means of a regular analytic function, or by Perlin-noise-based functions, possibly combined across multiple scales. In both cases, the reference surface constitutes the continuous model to be approximated.

The problem under study consists in constructing an approximating surface \(\widehat{\Sigma}\), defined piecewise, obtained by subdividing the domain into a rectangular grid and assigning to each cell a bilinear Coons patch. The theoretical interest of this construction lies in relating three distinct but closely connected aspects: the local structure of the boundary data, the global quality of the approximation, and the continuity of the resulting surface.

2. Domain discretization

Let the rectangular domain be

\[ D = [a,b] \times [c,d]. \]

It is subdivided into a uniform grid by introducing \(N_x\) subdivisions along the \(x\)-direction and \(N_z\) subdivisions along the \(z\)-direction. The grid nodes are therefore

\[ x_i = a + i\,\Delta x, \qquad i = 0,\dots,N_x, \]

\[ z_j = c + j\,\Delta z, \qquad j = 0,\dots,N_z, \]

where

\[ \Delta x = \frac{b-a}{N_x}, \qquad \Delta z = \frac{d-c}{N_z}. \]

Each pair of consecutive intervals defines a rectangular cell

\[ D_{ij} = [x_i,x_{i+1}] \times [z_j,z_{j+1}], \]

with \(i = 0,\dots,N_x-1\) and \(j = 0,\dots,N_z-1\).

The discretization of the domain makes it possible to replace a global problem, defined over the whole rectangle \(D\), with a family of local problems, each defined on a single cell. The overall approximation is therefore obtained by assembling the local solutions.

3. Local parametrization of a cell

For each cell \(D_{ij}\), an affine parametrization onto the unit square \([0,1]^2\) is introduced. If \((x,z) \in D_{ij}\), the local parameters \(u,v \in [0,1]\) are defined by

\[ u = \frac{x-x_i}{x_{i+1}-x_i}, \qquad v = \frac{z-z_j}{z_{j+1}-z_j}. \]

In this way, every cell is mapped to the same standard parametric domain, and the construction of the patch can be expressed in a uniform form, independently of the position of the cell in the global domain.

4. Boundary data

In order to construct a Coons patch on a cell \(D_{ij}\), one considers the four boundary curves obtained by restricting the reference surface to the edges of the rectangle. These are the parametrized curves

\[ C_0(u) = \bigl(x(u),\, f(x(u),z_j),\, z_j\bigr), \]

\[ C_1(u) = \bigl(x(u),\, f(x(u),z_{j+1}),\, z_{j+1}\bigr), \]

\[ D_0(v) = \bigl(x_i,\, f(x_i,z(v)),\, z(v)\bigr), \]

\[ D_1(v) = \bigl(x_{i+1},\, f(x_{i+1},z(v)),\, z(v)\bigr), \]

where

\[ x(u) = (1-u)x_i + ux_{i+1}, \qquad z(v) = (1-v)z_j + vz_{j+1}. \]

These four curves constitute the interpolation data for the patch. By construction, they coincide at the vertices of the cell. Indeed, if one denotes

\[ P_{00} = (x_i, f(x_i,z_j), z_j), \qquad P_{10} = (x_{i+1}, f(x_{i+1},z_j), z_j), \]

\[ P_{01} = (x_i, f(x_i,z_{j+1}), z_{j+1}), \qquad P_{11} = (x_{i+1}, f(x_{i+1},z_{j+1}), z_{j+1}), \]

one has the compatibility conditions

\[ C_0(0)=D_0(0)=P_{00}, \qquad C_0(1)=D_1(0)=P_{10}, \]

\[ C_1(0)=D_0(1)=P_{01}, \qquad C_1(1)=D_1(1)=P_{11}. \]

This condition is necessary for the interpolating surface to be well defined.

5. Definition of the bilinear Coons patch

Given four compatible boundary curves, the bilinear Coons patch is the parametric surface

\[ P : [0,1]^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3 \]

defined by

\[ P(u,v) = (1-v)C_0(u) + vC_1(u) + (1-u)D_0(v) + uD_1(v) - B(u,v), \]

where \(B(u,v)\) is the bilinear correction term.

This formula can be interpreted as the sum of two linear interpolations: one obtained by interpolating the horizontal boundary curves along the \(v\)-direction, the other obtained by interpolating the vertical boundary curves along the \(u\)-direction. Since this sum counts the corner contributions twice, an appropriate correction term must be subtracted.

6. Bilinear correction term

The correction term is the bilinear surface built from the four vertices of the cell:

\[ B(u,v) = (1-u)(1-v)P_{00} + u(1-v)P_{10} + (1-u)vP_{01} + uvP_{11}. \]

From an algebraic point of view, \(B(u,v)\) is the bilinear interpolation of the corner points. From a geometric point of view, it represents the common contribution of the two previous linear interpolations, which must be subtracted in order to avoid duplication.

7. Interpolation properties

The fundamental property of the Coons patch is the exact interpolation of the assigned boundaries. Indeed, by substituting \(v=0\) or \(v=1\), one obtains

\[ P(u,0)=C_0(u), \qquad P(u,1)=C_1(u), \]

while by setting \(u=0\) or \(u=1\), one gets

\[ P(0,v)=D_0(v), \qquad P(1,v)=D_1(v). \]

It follows that the patch reconstructs exactly the four boundary curves from which it is defined. This property is the reason why Coons patches constitute a natural tool in geometric modeling: they allow the interior of a surface to be determined from the sole information available on its boundary.

However, this exactness only concerns the boundary. In general, nothing guarantees that the interior of the patch coincides with the restriction of the original surface to the same cell.

8. Construction of the global approximating surface

The overall approximation of the reference surface is obtained by associating to each cell \(D_{ij}\) the corresponding Coons patch \(P_{ij}(u,v)\). One then defines a piecewise surface \(\widehat{\Sigma}\) whose local expression on each cell is

\[ \widehat{\Sigma}_{ij}(x,z) = P_{ij}(u(x),v(z)). \]

Symbolically, the approximating surface may be written as

\[ \widehat{\Sigma} = \bigcup_{i,j} \widehat{\Sigma}_{ij}. \]

The construction is therefore piecewise: each patch depends only on its own cell and on the corresponding boundary data, while the union of all patches yields a surface defined over the whole domain.

9. Continuity of the constructed surface

Since two adjacent patches share the same geometric boundary, the resulting surface is continuous. In analytic terms, it is at least \(C^0\). Therefore, no jumps in height or spatial discontinuities arise between one cell and the next.

Continuity of first derivatives, on the other hand, is not guaranteed in general. The partial derivatives along shared edges may depend on the internal structure of the two neighboring patches and may fail to coincide. The global surface is therefore not, in general, \(C^1\).

This is a theoretically relevant point. Coons patches provide exact boundary reconstruction and basic geometric continuity, but they do not automatically yield a smooth surface in the differential sense.

10. Boundary interpolation and interior approximation

The distinction between interpolation and approximation is central to the interpretation of the method. On the boundary of each cell, the Coons patch reproduces the reference surface exactly, since its edges are sampled directly from it. Inside the cell, by contrast, reconstruction is carried out solely through blending of the boundary data.

It follows that, except in special cases, the interior of the patch does not coincide exactly with the restriction of the original surface. The method uses only a limited amount of information: it knows the behavior of the surface along the edges, but it has no independent information about the interior.

11. Cases of exact reconstruction

There are situations in which reconstruction by means of Coons patches is exact. In particular, if the reference surface, restricted to a given cell, is compatible with the bilinear form determined by its boundaries, then the patch coincides with it at every point of the cell.

More generally, if the graph of the function is locally affine, or if the interior dependence is completely determined by the boundary data according to the bilinear model, then no approximation error appears. In nonlinear cases, by contrast, the difference between the actual surface and the reconstructed one tends to increase with the local curvature and complexity.

12. Pointwise approximation error

Denoting by \(f(x,z)\) the height of the reference surface and by \(\widehat{f}(x,z)\) the height of the approximated surface, the pointwise error is defined as

\[ e(x,z) = \lvert f(x,z) - \widehat{f}(x,z) \rvert. \]

This quantity measures the vertical discrepancy between the original surface and the reconstructed one. This is a natural choice in the case of surfaces represented as graphs of functions, since both surfaces are compared over the same point of the planar domain.

13. Global error measures

In order to obtain a synthetic evaluation of the approximation quality, the error is sampled over a finite set of points in the domain and summarized through several global quantities.

The mean error is defined by

\[ E_{\mathrm{mean}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} e_k, \]

where \(e_k\) is the error at the \(k\)-th sampled point and \(N\) is the total number of samples.

The maximum error is given by

\[ E_{\mathrm{max}} = \max_{1 \le k \le N} e_k, \]

and measures the worst observed discrepancy.

The root mean square error, or RMSE, is defined by

\[ E_{\mathrm{RMSE}} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} e_k^2 }. \]

Unlike the mean absolute error, the RMSE assigns greater weight to large errors, and is therefore particularly useful when one wishes to emphasize regions where the approximation fails more markedly.

14. Dependence of the error on discretization

One of the most significant aspects of the problem concerns the dependence of the error on the fineness of the grid. If the numbers of subdivisions \(N_x\) and \(N_z\) increase, the cells become smaller, and each Coons patch operates on a reduced portion of the surface. Under these conditions, interior reconstruction generally tends to become more accurate.

From an intuitive point of view, a sufficiently regular surface, when observed at progressively smaller scales, appears increasingly simple. Consequently, a local reconstruction based solely on boundary data tends to improve as the discretization is refined.

15. Complexity of the reference surface

The difficulty of the problem also depends on the nature of the reference surface. Regular, low-spatial-frequency surfaces, with smooth variations and an almost sinusoidal behavior, are generally easier to approximate with a limited number of patches. By contrast, noise-generated surfaces, especially when combining multiple detail scales, exhibit stronger local variations and make the approximation significantly harder.

16. Internal sampling of the patch and mesh representation

From a mathematical point of view, the Coons patch is a continuous parametric surface over the unit square. In order to be rendered visually, it must be sampled on an internal grid of parameter values \((u,v)\) and converted into a triangular mesh.

The so-called internal patch resolution does not change the definition of the surface, but only the way in which it is discretized for visualization purposes. A higher resolution produces a smoother and more detailed graphical representation; a lower resolution makes the triangular structure of the mesh more evident.

17. Interpretation of the error map

The application associates a color encoding with the pointwise error, thus producing a heatmap defined over the domain of the approximated surface. This representation introduces no new mathematical content, but serves an important analytical purpose: it makes the spatial distribution of the error immediately visible.

Nearly planar regions or regions of low curvature tend to be approximated better and therefore correspond to lower error values; conversely, areas with strong slope variations, ridges, valleys, or noisy detail show higher values.

18. Overall theoretical meaning

Taken as a whole, the problem under study constitutes an elementary but significant example of local geometric approximation. Coons patches make it possible to reconstruct a surface from boundary information, and for this very reason they clearly reveal both the limitations and the strengths of a local interpolation approach.

On the one hand, the method guarantees a simple construction, geometrically coherent and continuous in position; on the other hand, it does not in general ensure either exactness in the interior or continuity of first derivatives between adjacent patches. The resulting surface is therefore a good piecewise approximation, but not a smooth surface in the strong sense of the term.

19. Conclusion

The construction by means of bilinear Coons patches provides a natural procedure for approximating a surface defined over a rectangular domain. The method is based on subdividing the domain into cells, extracting boundary curves from each cell, and reconstructing the interior through parametric blending. The global surface is obtained as the union of local patches, continuous in position but not necessarily in derivatives.

Approximation error arises from the fact that the interior of each patch is determined solely by its boundaries and not by a complete knowledge of the original surface. Refining the grid tends to reduce this error, whereas increasing the complexity of the surface tends to amplify it. Numerical and visual error analysis therefore make it possible to study rigorously both the effectiveness of the method and its limitations.
